# Luxury reference patterns

These are reusable craft patterns, not literal clones. Extract the intent.

## 1. Quiet premium

Examples: Chanel, Dior.

Use near-neutral backgrounds, strong monochrome imagery, and restrained motion.

### Reusable lesson

When the message is exclusive, clutter dilutes relevance.

## 2. Editorial craft

Examples: Hermès, Cartier.

Use generous margins, measured serif/sans pairing, and product photography as a
material texture, not as decoration.

### Reusable lesson

Premium craft is often communicated through rhythm and restraint.

## 3. Heritage continuity

Examples: Louis Vuitton, Cartier.

Reuse a recognizable emblem, material pattern, or legacy palette as the link
between screens.

### Reusable lesson

Continuity beats novelty in identity-led interfaces.

## 4. Controlled maximalism

Example: Gucci.

Even expressive brand systems still obey one dominant hierarchy.

### Reusable lesson

Maximalism is a controlled range, not visual chaos.

## 5. Warm identity accent

Examples: Hermès, Louis Vuitton.

Use a single accent color to signal brand identity, not to mark every call to
action.

### Reusable lesson

One accent should belong to the brand, not to a template.

## 6. Product-as-craft

Examples: Hermès, Gucci, Cartier.

Use close-up texture, proportion, and material cues to show why the object is
unique.

### Reusable lesson

Show craft evidence, not claim it in an adjective.

## When not to use

Do not use luxury aesthetics as a default for:

1. Dashboards.
2. Administrative tools.
3. Time-critical operational interfaces.
4. Internal admin screens where speed and trust are more important than mood.
